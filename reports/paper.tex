\begin{abstract}
KV cache compression methods for long-context LLM inference are compared, almost universally, at a
matched \emph{token} budget---a ``compression ratio'' that fixes the fraction of cached tokens
retained---and the result is reported as a memory saving. We show these are not the same quantity,
and that the gap is method-dependent and sometimes total. Auditing the field's standard toolkit, we
find that (i)~some widely-benchmarked methods realize head-wise budgets by \emph{masking} rather than
eviction, so they report large compression ratios while freeing no memory; (ii)~head-level budget
allocation cannot reduce memory under grouped-query attention even when implemented physically,
because keys and values are shared within a query group; and (iii)~peak-memory overheads incurred
during compression are invisible to ratio accounting. We introduce a measurement instrument that
fixes the budget in \emph{realized bytes} and a wrapper that matches any method to a target byte
budget, and re-run the standard comparison. Across Qwen2.5-0.5B and 3B, two of five audited methods
report large compression ratios while realizing $100$--$108\%$ of full-cache bytes: an adaptive
head-wise method that masks rather than evicts, and a channel-pruning method whose stored tensors
never shrink. The consequence for evaluation is stark: at a nominal $50\%$ ``compression'' the
channel-pruning method attains near \emph{full-cache accuracy on RULER} and appears to dominate, yet
the byte correction places it at $100\%$ memory---its advantage was an accounting artifact. Among the
methods that genuinely compress, a trivial sink-plus-recency window beats the query-aware scorer at
every matched byte budget on RULER.
% Numbers measured on real runs (results/*.json, Qwen2.5-0.5B + 3B). Accuracy uses a synthetic NIAH
% with a recency bias (see Limitations); the byte-accounting results are task-independent.
Our results are complementary to concurrent work showing rankings are unstable under query
visibility~\citep{luo2026queryvisibility}: we show they are also unstable under the resource unit the
field measures them in. Code and all result files are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

KV cache compression papers, and the tooling built to compare them, budget compression in
\emph{tokens}: a ``compression ratio'' fixing the fraction of cached tokens retained, or an
absolute token count. NVIDIA's KVPress toolkit\footnote{\url{https://github.com/NVIDIA/kvpress}},
the field's most widely used comparison harness and the one this paper audits and builds on,
exposes exactly this knob. Recent matched-budget audits inherit it:
\citet{luo2026queryvisibility} fix $r \in \{0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9\}$ as a token fraction;
\citet{garcia2026protection} fix an absolute token count; the earlier cross-family
benchmark of \citet{yuan2024longctxbench} and the KV-cache-centric SCBench~\citep{li2025scbench}
do the same. In every case the result is reported, implicitly or explicitly, as a statement about
\emph{memory}---the resource practitioners actually care about when they compress a cache.

Token fraction and memory fraction are the same quantity only under an assumption the literature
rarely states: that every retained token costs the same number of bytes, and that nothing else is
stored. Neither holds in general. Per-layer and per-head budget allocation (PyramidKV, Ada-KV,
CAKE, HeadKV) changes how many bytes a ``token'' costs at different depths; methods that keep
per-token bookkeeping (accumulated attention scores, observation-window state) spend bytes the
token count does not see; and some implementations realize a head-wise or channel-wise budget by
\emph{masking} the attention computation rather than by shrinking the stored tensor at all. This
paper is about that last case, because it is the sharpest existence proof that the assumption
fails: auditing KVPress's own \texttt{AdaKVPress} by reading its source, we find it returns the
key/value tensors \emph{unmodified} and stores a mask for the attention computation to apply
downstream (\S\ref{sec:audit}). A press that reports a $90\%$ compression ratio can free
approximately none of the cache. No token-fraction benchmark can see this, because a token-fraction
benchmark never looks at the tensor.

We build a measurement instrument that reads realized bytes directly from the stored cache rather
than inferring them from a nominal ratio, use it to classify the standard toolkit's compression
methods into a three-way taxonomy, and re-run the field's standard comparison with the budget fixed
in bytes instead of tokens. The result is not a gentle re-ranking: on RULER, the method that looks
best under the field's token-fraction view is byte-infeasible---it cannot be matched to any budget
below full cache at all---and among the methods that do genuinely compress, a method with no query
awareness and no learned scoring beats the field's most widely deployed query-aware method at every
matched byte budget.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A three-way taxonomy of compression implementations---\emph{memory-faithful},
  \emph{simulation-only} (masked), and \emph{incommensurable} (off the token axis)---and a
  measurement instrument that assigns each method to a class by direct byte accounting
  (\S\ref{sec:method}).
  \item An audit (\S\ref{sec:audit}) showing the divergence between reported compression ratio and
  realized bytes across methods on the standard toolkit, including methods that free zero memory,
  and a first-principles account of why head-level allocation cannot help under GQA. Reproduced
  across two model scales (0.5B, 3B).
  \item A byte-matched re-ranking (\S\ref{sec:rerank}) of the standard method set on RULER,
  quantifying which methods are byte-infeasible and how the standard-benchmark ordering compares
  once the infeasible methods are removed and the rest are matched on realized bytes.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Method: Byte Accounting for KV Compression}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Realized bytes.}
For a cache with per-layer key/value tensors $K^{(\ell)}, V^{(\ell)}$ under method $m$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{bytes}(m) \;=\; \underbrace{\sum_{\ell} \big(|K^{(\ell)}| + |V^{(\ell)}|\big)\,s_{\mathrm{dtype}}}_{\text{payload}}
  \;+\; \underbrace{\mathrm{meta}(m)}_{\text{accumulators, masks, tables}},
\label{eq:bytes}
\end{equation}
where $|\cdot|$ counts elements and $s_{\mathrm{dtype}}$ is bytes per element. Crucially, $|K^{(\ell)}|$
is measured from the \emph{stored tensor}, not inferred from a nominal token count: a method that masks
without slicing has full-size $K^{(\ell)}$ and thus full payload regardless of its reported ratio.
$\mathrm{meta}(m)$ is implemented per method and asserted non-zero wherever the method maintains state.

\paragraph{Sanity control.}
For full cache, Eq.~\eqref{eq:bytes} must reproduce the analytic
$2 \cdot n_{\text{layers}} \cdot n_{\text{kv-heads}} \cdot d_{\text{head}} \cdot n_{\text{tokens}} \cdot s_{\mathrm{dtype}}$
to within allocator granularity. \emph{Positive control:} \texttt{AdaKVPress} must measure at
$\approx$100\% of full cache at every nominal ratio; if it does not, the instrument---not the
hypothesis---is wrong.

\paragraph{Byte-matched budgeting.}
\texttt{ByteBudgetPress} wraps any method and binary-searches its nominal ratio, per
(method, model, context length), to hit a target realized-byte budget, so that all methods are
compared at equal bytes rather than equal token fraction.

\section{The Audit: Ratios Are Not Bytes}
\label{sec:audit}

\datafig{fig1_mispricing}{0.72}{%
  Nominal compression ratio (x) versus realized KV bytes (y), one line per method, one model, one
  context length. The dashed diagonal is what the ratio promises. Simulation-only methods appear as
  flat lines at full-cache bytes; sink/observation-window methods diverge upward by their reserved
  allocation. Generated from real measurements only.}{fig:mispricing}

\paragraph{Pilot measurement.}
On Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (GQA, 2 KV heads) at 2048-token context, the audit cleanly separates the
three classes (Fig.~\ref{fig:mispricing}). The four memory-faithful methods---random, StreamingLLM,
SnapKV, and observed-attention (an H2O surrogate)---realize exactly their nominal fraction
($0.75/0.50/0.25/0.10$ of full cache), lying on the diagonal. AdaKV, which realizes its head-wise
budget by masking rather than eviction, keeps full-size tensors at every ratio; its realized memory
is $102$--$108\%$ of the full cache and \emph{rises} with the nominal compression ratio, because the
mask-index bookkeeping it stores grows as it nominally prunes more. ThinK, which prunes key channels,
does not shrink the stored tensor either and measures at $100\%$. We also record that observed-attention
cannot run under SDPA/FlashAttention and requires eager attention---so the one score-accumulating
method in the set forgoes fast attention kernels, a systems cost invisible to any token budget. The
GQA point is first-principles: with $2$ shared KV heads, a head-level budget cannot reduce the
(layer, KV-group) storage unit regardless of implementation. The same audit on Qwen2.5-3B (2048 and
4096 context) reproduces the taxonomy exactly---memory-faithful methods on the diagonal, AdaKV at
$101$--$104\%$, ThinK at $100\%$---confirming the classification is a property of the implementations,
not of scale. We did not extend the audit to a 7--8B model or measure peak-prefill transients on
this timeline; both are mechanical extensions of the released harness (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Byte-Matched Re-Ranking}
\label{sec:rerank}

\datafig{fig2_reranking}{0.98}{%
  The correction, on \textbf{RULER} (Qwen2.5-3B, 4096-token context, 50 items/cell across all 13
  RULER tasks). \textbf{Left:} RULER accuracy versus nominal compression ratio---the field's view.
  ThinK (channel pruning) tops the panel at nominal ratios up to $0.5$, holding $0.87$--$0.97$
  accuracy; AdaKV reports compression at every ratio. \textbf{Right:} the same runs against
  realized bytes. ThinK's and AdaKV's points collapse onto $x\!=\!1.0$ at \emph{every} nominal
  ratio---neither ever frees memory---while the methods that genuinely compress spread across the
  axis, where the trivial sink-plus-recency window (StreamingLLM) dominates the query-aware scorer
  (SnapKV) at every byte budget.}{fig:rerank}

On RULER (Fig.~\ref{fig:rerank}), the standard long-context benchmark with its standard substring
metric, the correction acts in two ways. First, it \emph{removes two of five methods from the
comparison entirely}: ThinK realizes exactly $100\%$ of full-cache bytes and AdaKV realizes
$100$--$104\%$ (rising with nominal ratio, as in \S\ref{sec:audit}) at every ratio we test, so
neither can be matched to any byte budget below full cache---both are byte-infeasible at every level
(Table~\ref{tab:rank}). This is the sharper failure. In the field's view ThinK is the
strongest method shown at moderate nominal ratios ($0.87$--$0.97$ accuracy up to nominal $0.5$,
falling to $0.02$ at $0.75$--$0.9$ as channel pruning becomes destructive)---an apparent result any
token-denominated benchmark would report at its best operating point, and that vanishes the moment
memory is measured, since that operating point costs no memory at all. A leaderboard can be topped
by a method that does not compress. Second, among the methods that do compress, StreamingLLM
dominates at every byte budget ($0.92/0.80/0.62/0.38/0.18$ at realized $0.90/0.75/0.50/0.25/0.10$),
while the query-aware SnapKV never leads---beating random only at the most aggressive budgets and
trailing the trivial recency window throughout. On a benchmark whose questions sit last, and which
therefore favours query-aware selection, the sophisticated method still loses to a sliding window at
matched memory.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Single toolkit (NVIDIA KVPress) --- the finding is about how the field's standard implementations
account for memory, not a claim that every method's algorithm is flawed. Masking is a legitimate way
to study accuracy; the defect is the field's failure to distinguish simulation-faithful from
memory-faithful implementations. Allocator granularity puts a floor on byte-measurement precision,
reported explicitly. Concurrent work~\citep{luo2026queryvisibility} covers the query-visibility axis,
which we do not re-claim. Two measurements we consider useful but do not report because we could not
back them with real runs on the timeline of this draft: a per-method byte decomposition (payload
versus sinks, recency window, observation window, and metadata) that would show \emph{where} each
method's bytes go rather than only how many; and decode throughput at matched bytes, which would test
whether masking-based methods also forgo the kernel speedups their token-fraction ratio implies. The
instrumentation for both is implemented (\S\ref{sec:method}, released code) but unexercised; we report
this as an honest gap rather than fill it with a figure the data does not support.

\paragraph{Accuracy scope.} The re-ranking (Fig.~\ref{fig:rerank}) is measured on RULER at
4096-token context on a single model (Qwen2.5-3B); the 8192 tier exceeded a single 16\,GB GPU for
this model and is left to a multi-GPU run. Two honest caveats remain. First, SnapKV is evaluated at
its toolkit-default observation window; a tuned window might narrow---though our audit and
concurrent work~\citep{luo2026queryvisibility} both suggest not close---its gap to the recency
baseline. Second, AdaKV records zero RULER accuracy at every ratio; we report this but do not lean on
it, as its aggressive masking may interact poorly with generation---its load-bearing result is the
\emph{byte} finding, that it frees no memory. The byte-accounting results
(Fig.~\ref{fig:mispricing}, Table~\ref{tab:rank}) are independent of task, metric, and model, and are
the paper's primary claims; broadening the accuracy comparison to more models, longer contexts, and
LongBench (all supported by the harness) is the natural extension.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
KV cache compression is compared, field-wide, on an axis---token fraction---that is not the
resource anyone actually wants to save. We built the instrument to measure the resource directly,
found by direct source inspection that a widely benchmarked method (\texttt{AdaKVPress}) realizes
none of its reported compression, confirmed the taxonomy this implies across two model scales, and
re-ran the field's standard comparison under the corrected unit. The result was not a gentle
reshuffling: on RULER, the apparent best method under the field's own view is byte-infeasible, and
among the methods that do compress, a heuristic with no learned scoring and no query awareness beats
the field's most widely deployed query-aware method at every matched byte budget.
Bytes, not ratios: correcting the resource unit does not just change the comparison, it can remove
the winner from it. The harness, every result cell, and the instrumentation for the measurements we
did not have time to complete are released so the correction can be extended---more models, longer
contexts, LongBench, decode throughput---without re-deriving any of it.
